India vs China Garlic: Which Origin Should You Buy From?

A side-by-side comparison of the world's two biggest garlic producers, growing regions, variety, price, grading, export capacity, and the buying scenarios where each one wins.

Published: October 11, 2026  |  Reading time: 12 minutes  |  By ExCrop (Heneng International Trade Group, Shandong)

Quick Answer: India and China grow more garlic than anyone else, but they serve completely different markets. China exports around 2 million tonnes a year (about 80% of global trade) at competitive FOB prices with strict size grading from 4.5cm to 6.5cm+. India consumes nearly all of its own garlic at some of the world's highest domestic prices and exports only small volumes. For bulk import programs, China is the practical choice; for buyers inside or near India, local garlic is worth considering when the season aligns.

  • Bulk standardized import volumes, buy Chinese (reefer, FOB Qingdao, MOQ 28MT)
  • Small pungent purple-skin bulbs for South Asian palates, buy Indian when in season
  • Indian harvest: roughly December-March; Chinese harvest: June, then cold storage year-round
  • Chinese garlic is graded by diameter; Indian grading is looser and more variable

India and China together grow the majority of the world's garlic, yet the two origins could hardly be more different as sourcing options. Buyers in South Asia, the Gulf, Africa, and Southeast Asia frequently weigh them against each other, and the right answer depends on what you are actually buying: bulk commodity volume for a wholesale program, or specific sensory characteristics for a local market. This guide compares both origins honestly, including the cases where Indian garlic is the smarter buy.

Growing Regions and Supply Seasons

India's garlic heartland is Madhya Pradesh, with the Mandsaur-Neemuch belt as the single most important production cluster; Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh add significant volume. Indian garlic is planted mainly from October to November and harvested from roughly December through March, depending on region and elevation. Storage is largely ambient or simple ventilated structures, so domestic marketing and consumption concentrate within a few months of harvest.

China's export supply is anchored by Shandong province, supported by Henan and other regions. Garlic is planted in October, harvested in June, cured for 2-3 weeks, and then placed in industrial cold stores at -2 to -3 degrees Celsius, which keeps it export-ready for 8-10 months. The result is year-round shipment capability with a stable quality profile.

The calendars are largely complementary. Indian garlic is freshest and most available in the first half of the calendar year; Chinese garlic peaks in fresh supply from July to September and relies on cold storage for the rest of the year. For an importer in, say, Dubai or Kuala Lumpur, this means Indian garlic can cover spring months when it is competitive locally, while Chinese reefer containers cover everything else. Variety, Size, and Appearance

The two origins deliver visibly different products:

  • Indian garlic skews small, with a high share of purple- or purple-striped-skinned varieties. Cloves are typically smaller and fewer per bulb than Chinese white garlic, and the flavor is sharper and hotter, which suits South Asian cooking, where garlic is often pounded raw into masalas and pastes. Bulb size is more variable within a lot.
  • Chinese garlic for export is overwhelmingly white-skinned, in two main grades: Normal White Garlic (with faint purple stripes on the outer skin, the workhorse of world trade) and Pure White Garlic (uniformly bright white, a premium retail product). Bulbs run larger, and the trade is organized around diameter grades: 4.5cm, 5.0cm, 5.5cm, 6.0cm, and 6.5cm and above. Flavor is full but more balanced and less aggressively hot than typical Indian purple varieties.

If your market equates "good garlic" with fierce pungency, Indian purple garlic has real fans. If your market buys on appearance, uniformity, and size (supermarket shelving, food service prepping, industrial processing), Chinese white garlic is engineered for exactly that. For a deeper dive into appearance differences, see Purple Skin vs White Garlic and our garlic size specification guide.

Price Comparison: Two Different Worlds

This is where the comparison becomes lopsided. Indian domestic garlic prices are, in most years, among the highest in the world. Strong and steadily growing domestic demand, fragmented smallholder supply, limited cold storage, and periodic weather shocks (late rains, unseasonal heat) regularly push Indian wholesale prices to levels several times the Chinese farmgate equivalent. When Indian prices spike, the country sometimes even turns net importer.

Chinese export garlic, by contrast, is a globally competitive commodity. Massive scale, industrial cold storage, deep-water export logistics via Qingdao, and intense competition among exporters keep FOB China levels highly competitive on world markets. For any buyer outside India's immediate neighborhood, Chinese garlic typically lands at a substantially lower cost per kilogram for comparable usability, even after ocean freight.

Grading and Standardization

This is an underrated difference. Chinese export garlic is traded against a strict, universally understood calibre system: bulbs are graded by minimum diameter into 4.5cm, 5.0cm, 5.5cm, 6.0cm, and 6.5cm+ classes, and a contract that says "5.5cm pure white" means something precise and checkable with a ring gauge at loading. Packaging is likewise standardized, mesh bags or cartons, typically loaded loose or palletized into 40HQ reefers holding about 28 metric tons.

Indian garlic moves predominantly through domestic mandi channels where grading is coarser, often by variety and general appearance rather than measured diameter. When Indian garlic is exported, it is frequently a byproduct of domestic surplus rather than a purpose-graded export lot, so consistency from shipment to shipment is harder to guarantee. For importers supplying supermarkets or processing lines that need a repeatable specification, this difference alone usually settles the question. Export Capacity: 2 Million Tonnes vs. Domestic First

Scale is the final decisive factor. China exports on the order of 2 million tonnes of garlic per year, roughly 80% of all garlic traded internationally, to more than 100 countries, backed by dedicated cold storage measured in millions of tonnes and the world's largest container shipping operations. An importer can contract dozens of 40HQ reefers a month, twelve months a year, and expect consistent execution.

India is simultaneously one of the world's largest garlic producers and one of its largest garlic consumers. The domestic market absorbs nearly the entire crop, export volumes are comparatively small and opportunistic, and in short-crop years India has historically restricted or banned exports to protect local supply, occasionally importing garlic itself. No large import program anywhere in the world currently runs on Indian garlic as its primary origin; the volumes simply are not there.

That is not a criticism of Indian garlic as a product, it is a statement about market structure. India grows excellent garlic for Indian food. China grows garlic for the world.

Which Buyers Should Choose Which Origin?

After the comparisons above, the decision framework is straightforward:

  • Buy Chinese garlic if: you import bulk volumes (28MT container loads), you need a fixed calibre specification (e.g., 5.5cm normal white), you supply supermarkets or food processors, your destination is outside India's short-haul logistics zone, or you need supply twelve months a year. The combination of price, grading, and reliability is unmatched.
  • Consider Indian garlic if: you are located in India or a neighboring market, your customers specifically want small pungent purple-skin bulbs, you buy in the December-March fresh season, and you can work with looser grading and shorter shelf life. In these conditions, shorter supply chains can beat ocean freight from China.
  • Consider both if: you operate in the Gulf or Southeast Asia, where both origins compete, and you can arbitrage seasonally between Indian fresh-crop windows and Chinese cold stored supply.

One more practical note: even buyers who prefer Indian flavor sometimes blend sourcing, Indian garlic for the fresh spring window, Chinese garlic as the year-round base supply, because a program that depends entirely on a thin exportable surplus is a program with built-in supply risk.

Logistics and Documentation: The Practical Gap

Beyond the field and the price, the two origins differ in how easily they can actually reach your warehouse:

  • Cold chain. Chinese garlic moves through an industrial system: cold stores at -2 to -3 degrees Celsius, reefer containers set to the same temperature, and set-point discipline written into bookings. Indian garlic moves mostly ambient, so long-distance buyers absorb the full sprouting and spoilage risk, see our sprouting prevention guide for what that costs.
  • Export infrastructure. Qingdao is one of the world's largest container ports with weekly sailings to virtually every garlic-importing region. Indian garlic exports depend on more fragmented channels and smaller volumes, which complicates container-scale purchases.
  • Trade stability. Chinese garlic faces its share of tariff and anti-dumping measures in specific markets, but the underlying flow is continuous. Indian garlic availability can be interrupted by domestic policy in tight years. Importers should check current measures for their own market before committing, see garlic import regulations by country.

None of this makes Indian garlic a bad product; it makes it a local product. The further your market sits from India's domestic logistics orbit, the more the practical gaps compound.

The Bottom Line

"Which garlic is better" has no absolute answer, but "which garlic is better for a given buyer" almost always does. For standardized bulk import programs, the kind that move in reefer containers and land on wholesale and supermarket shelves, Chinese garlic's grading discipline, price competitiveness, cold chain, and 2-million-tonne export capacity make it the default global choice. Indian garlic is a strong regional product whose domestic market absorbs nearly all of it, and it shines where local taste and short logistics matter more than uniformity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Chinese garlic better than Indian garlic?
They are different products. Chinese garlic is larger, whiter, uniformly graded and available year-round in bulk; Indian garlic is smaller, often purple-skinned, hotter in flavor and best consumed close to where it is grown. For import programs, Chinese garlic is the standard choice; for local South Asian markets, Indian garlic is often preferred.
Why doesn't India export more garlic?
India's enormous domestic demand absorbs nearly the entire crop at high local prices, leaving little surplus for export. In tight years, export restrictions or imports have even occurred. Export infrastructure like industrial cold storage and grading is also far less developed than China's.
Is Chinese garlic cheaper than Indian garlic?
In most years, yes, substantially, once you compare comparable usability. Indian domestic prices are among the world's highest, while Chinese export FOB levels are globally competitive. Exact gaps vary seasonally, so compare live quotes before ordering.
Which garlic is spicier, Indian or Chinese?
Indian garlic, especially the purple-skinned varieties common around Mandsaur and other Madhya Pradesh belts, is generally sharper and hotter. Chinese white garlic delivers a full, more balanced pungency that suits a wider range of cuisines and processing applications.
Can I mix Indian and Chinese garlic in my sourcing program?
Yes, and many Gulf and Southeast Asian traders do exactly that: Indian garlic during its December-March fresh window when local logistics favor it, and Chinese cold stored garlic as the year-round base supply. The complementary calendars make blending practical.
