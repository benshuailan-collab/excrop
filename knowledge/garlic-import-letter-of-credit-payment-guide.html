Letter of Credit for Garlic Imports: Complete L/C Payment Guide

How garlic buyers can use L/C at sight, T/T 30/70, and D/P safely: payment method comparison, the 6-step letter of credit process, common discrepancies, banking fees, and practical advice for new and repeat buyers.

Published: October 11, 2026 | Reading time: 13 min

Quick Answer: The three most common payment methods in the China garlic trade are T/T 30/70 (30% deposit, 70% against B/L copy), L/C at sight (bank pays against compliant shipping documents), and D/P (documents released only after payment). L/C at sight offers the best protection for large first-time transactions — the seller typically receives funds within 5-10 business days after presenting clean documents, and each party normally bears its own bank's charges. ExCrop accepts L/C at sight from reputable banks for shipments of 1 x 40HQ reefer container (28MT) and above.

Payment terms are one of the most negotiated — and most misunderstood — parts of any garlic import contract. A single 40HQ reefer container of Normal White or Pure White garlic from Shandong can represent tens of thousands of US dollars, and the buyer is paying weeks before the container arrives at ports like Jeddah, Jebel Ali, Santos, or Durban. Choosing the right payment structure protects both sides: the buyer does not want to wire money and receive nothing, and the seller does not want to ship a container and not get paid.

This guide explains how garlic payments actually work in practice: the three dominant payment methods compared side by side, exactly how a letter of credit flows from opening to final payment, the clauses that cause 90% of L/C discrepancies, and what payment terms make sense depending on whether you are a first-time or repeat buyer.

Why Payment Terms Matter So Much in the Garlic Trade

Garlic is a seasonal agricultural commodity. Prices move week by week during the storage season, and a shipment that sits unresolved in a payment dispute loses freshness every day. Unlike machinery or consumer goods, a delayed garlic container generates real losses: demurrage at destination ($75-150 per container per day at many ports), cold-chain risk if the reefer is unplugged, and quality deterioration if the cargo is stuck at -2°C to -3°C or warmer for too long.

This is why experienced garlic importers treat payment structure as a risk-management decision, not just a financing one. The right answer depends on three questions: How large is the transaction? Do you already trust this supplier? And does your bank offer affordable trade-finance products? The Three Main Payment Methods in Garlic Trade

Virtually every garlic export contract from China uses one of three payment structures — or a hybrid of them. Here is how each works and who carries the risk.

1. T/T 30/70 (Telegraphic Transfer)

The most common method in the garlic trade. The buyer wires a 30% deposit to confirm the order, and the seller starts production, booking the reefer container and preparing the cargo. The remaining 70% is paid after shipment, typically against a scanned copy of the bill of lading — before the original documents are released.

  • Advantages: Simple, fast, and cheap — bank fees are usually $30-100 per transfer. No document requirements beyond the invoice. The seller gets working capital to buy and pack the garlic.
  • Disadvantages: The 30% deposit is pure trust — it is unprotected if the supplier fails to perform. The buyer also pays 100% before seeing the goods or controlling original title documents in some variants.
  • Best for: Repeat business between established partners with a multi-year track record.

2. L/C at Sight (Letter of Credit)

The buyer's bank (issuing bank) guarantees payment when the seller presents a compliant set of shipping documents — bill of lading, invoice, packing list, phytosanitary certificate, and so on. "At sight" means payment is due immediately once documents are verified, rather than after 30/60/90 days.

  • Advantages: Bank-level protection for both sides. The seller ships knowing a bank stands behind the payment; the buyer's bank only releases funds against proof of shipment. Ideal for large first-time transactions.
  • Disadvantages: Higher banking fees than T/T (see FAQ below), stricter document discipline required, and 1-2 weeks of additional process around opening and amending the credit.
  • Best for: New trading relationships, high-value multi-container orders, and buyers whose banks offer cheap L/C lines.

3. D/P (Documents Against Payment)

The seller sends shipping documents through the banking channel. The buyer's bank releases the original documents (including the bill of lading needed to claim the container) only after the buyer pays. Cheaper than an L/C, but there is no bank guarantee — if the buyer refuses to pay, the seller is left with a container of garlic at a foreign port.

  • Advantages: Lower banking fees than an L/C; buyer does not pay before goods are shipped.
  • Disadvantages: The seller retains full non-payment risk. If the buyer walks away, the seller must find another buyer in that market or pay to re-ship the cargo. For this reason, many Chinese garlic exporters accept D/P only from buyers with prior history.
  • Best for: Second or third orders with a buyer who has already paid on time once.

How a Letter of Credit Actually Works: The 6-Step Process

Many buyers avoid L/Cs simply because nobody ever walked them through the mechanics. In practice, the process is a predictable relay between four parties: the buyer (applicant), the buyer's bank (issuing bank), the seller's bank (advising/negotiating bank), and the seller (beneficiary). Here is the full cycle for a garlic shipment from Qingdao:

  1. Step 1 — Sales contract agreed. Buyer and seller agree on price, quantity, specification (for example Pure White 5.5cm, 28MT in one 40HQ reefer at -2°C to -3°C), Incoterms (FOB Qingdao or CIF destination port), and L/C at sight as payment. The contract is the blueprint the L/C must mirror.
  2. Step 2 — Buyer opens the L/C. The buyer applies at its bank, pledging the L/C amount against its credit line or cash margin. The bank issues the credit, usually by SWIFT MT700, addressed to the seller's bank in China. Opening typically takes 3-7 business days.
  3. Step 3 — Advising bank checks and notifies the seller. The seller's bank verifies the authenticity of the SWIFT message and advises the credit to the seller. The seller reviews every clause — any error found now costs one amendment instead of a discrepancy later.
  4. Step 4 — Seller ships within the L/C window and prepares documents. The garlic is inspected, loaded at Qingdao, and the document set is assembled: commercial invoice, packing list, full set of clean bills of lading, phytosanitary certificate issued by China Customs, certificate of origin, and any certificates the L/C requires (fumigation, quality/weight). All documents must be consistent with each other and with the L/C word for word. For details on this document set, see our garlic import regulations by country guide and the container loading guide.
  5. Step 5 — Presentation and examination. The seller presents the documents to its bank within the presentation period (commonly 21 days after the bill of lading date and before L/C expiry). The negotiating bank examines the documents against the credit, typically within 3-5 business days, and forwards a clean set to the issuing bank.
  6. Step 6 — Payment (negotiation) and document release. Under an L/C at sight, the issuing bank honors payment — usually within 5-10 business days of clean presentation — and releases the original documents to the buyer, who uses the bill of lading to claim the container at the destination port. The buyer then reimburses its bank per its credit arrangement.

Note that the buyer pays its bank, not the seller directly — and the seller's protection ends at document compliance. An L/C is a payment instrument, not a quality guarantee: it proves the garlic was shipped, not that every bulb is perfect. That is why serious buyers combine L/C payment with a pre-shipment inspection by a third party such as SGS, BV, or Intertek.

Key L/C Clauses and the Discrepancies That Cause Refusals

Roughly half of first-time L/C presentations in agricultural trade contain at least one discrepancy, and under UCP 600 (the governing rules for letters of credit) the issuing bank may refuse discrepant documents. Each refusal costs time, amendment fees, and sometimes the deal. These are the clauses that deserve the most attention when you draft the credit:

  • Latest shipment date and expiry: Garlic booking, inspection, and loading take 7-14 days. If the L/C requires shipment "within 10 days of L/C issuance," a single reefer equipment shortage at Qingdao can blow the window. Leave realistic buffers, and set expiry in China (not the buyer's country) with a presentation period of at least 21 days after the B/L date.
  • Spelling and description consistency: The goods description on the invoice must mirror the L/C. If the credit says "Pure White Garlic 5.5cm up" and the invoice says "Pure White Garlic size 5.5cm+", that is a discrepancy. Copy the exact product description, including the HS code, from the contract into the L/C — and require your supplier to echo it.
  • Quantity and amount tolerance: A 40HQ reefer legally carries about 28MT of packed garlic, but the actual loaded weight varies by carton, moisture, and packing pattern — commonly within ±2-3%. If the L/C states exactly "28,000 kgs" and "USD 30,800" with no tolerance (using "about" or a +/-5% clause), a load of 27,540 kgs is discrepant and the whole payment can stall. Ask your supplier how it words quantities on final documents before the L/C is drafted.
  • Document dating logic: Documents must tell a believable story in the right order: inspection before shipment, phytosanitary certificate on or before the B/L date, insurance (under CIF) dated no later than the B/L date. Contradictory dates are a classic discrepancy that banks do catch.
  • Required documents list: Every additional certificate (fumigation, health, analysis) is another chance for a mismatch. Require exactly what your customs authority needs — no more. For the standard China garlic document set, see our article on the export inspection and phytosanitary process.
  • Partial shipments and transshipment: Many liner services from Qingdao to South America, West Africa, and the Middle East transship at hub ports. If the L/C prohibits transshipment under UCP 600, a hub relay on through bills of lading does not count as transshipment — but a straight "no transshipment allowed" clause can create needless refusals. Allow transshipment, and partial shipments if you may split volume across sailings.

A practical discipline that eliminates most discrepancies: have the supplier send a draft document set (invoice, packing list, draft B/L data) before the originals are issued, and compare every field against the L/C. Five minutes of checking "Pure White" vs "pure white," container numbers, and weights up front saves two weeks of refusal letters later.

Payment Advice: New Buyers vs Repeat Buyers

There is no single "best" payment method — there is a best method for your situation:

  • First-time buyers, single container: L/C at sight is the strongest option if your bank's fees are reasonable. It converts a leap-of-faith wire transfer into a document-controlled transaction, and it signals seriousness that good suppliers respond to. If an L/C is impractical for a small amount, start with a trial container on T/T 30/70 with a supplier whose export record you have verified, and order a pre-shipment inspection.
  • First-time buyers, multiple containers: Definitely use an L/C. The larger the amount, the more the L/C's protection outweighs its fees, and the issuing bank's document review adds a second set of eyes to the shipment.
  • Second and third orders: Once the first container has arrived on time and on spec, D/P becomes viable — the seller takes measured risk, and you save on L/C charges. This is the natural stepping stone between L/C and open T/T terms.
  • Established annual programs: Long-term importers running 2-6 containers per month typically settle on T/T 30/70 or even consolidated monthly payments. Speed and low fees win once trust is proven, but keep the discipline of paying against B/L copy so title documents are never held up over an overdue balance.

One warning that applies to every category: garlic prices move with the season, so avoid long gaps between paying a deposit and confirming the final balance basis — specify in the contract exactly which documents the balance is paid against, and in what currency. Frequently Asked Questions

Who pays the bank charges in a garlic L/C transaction?

Under standard practice, each party bears its own bank's fees: the buyer pays the issuing bank's charges for opening and amending the credit, and the seller pays the advising and negotiating bank's charges in China. This split is normally written into the L/C itself. Confirm the fee allocation in the sales contract before the credit is opened so nobody is surprised.

How long does it take the seller to receive payment under an L/C at sight?

Typically 5-10 business days after the seller presents a clean document set. The negotiating bank examines documents (3-5 business days), forwards them to the issuing bank, and the issuing bank honors the sight draft. Discrepant documents can stretch this to 2-4 weeks — or end in refusal — which is why document accuracy is the single biggest speed factor.

What is the most common L/C discrepancy in garlic shipments?

Quantity and weight mismatches. A 40HQ legally loads around 28MT of garlic, but actual loaded weight naturally varies by ±2-3% depending on packing and moisture. An L/C that fixes an exact quantity with no tolerance ("about" or ±5%) turns a normal variation into a discrepancy. Always include a tolerance clause.

Can I use an L/C for just one container of garlic?

Yes, but weigh the economics. L/C opening charges and negotiation fees are largely fixed, so they make more sense on larger amounts. For a single 28MT container, some buyers find the fees high relative to the cargo value and prefer T/T 30/70 with a third-party pre-shipment inspection. For two or more containers, or high-value Pure White 6.0cm+ orders, the L/C usually justifies itself.
