Dehydrated Garlic Flakes: The Complete Sourcing Guide for Bulk Buyers

Production process, quality specifications, packaging, pricing mechanics, HS classification, and inspection standards for importing bulk dehydrated garlic flakes from China.

Dehydrated garlic is the quiet backbone of the global food industry. While fresh garlic moves through produce channels, dehydrated flakes feed seasoning blends, instant noodle packets, soup bases, sausage products, spice mills, and food service kitchens on every continent. Because it is a processed ingredient rather than a fresh vegetable, sourcing it well requires a different checklist: processing standards, moisture control, microbiology, and packaging integrity matter more than harvest timing. This guide walks through everything a bulk buyer needs to know before the first container.

What Dehydrated Garlic Flakes Are and How They Are Made

Garlic flakes are simply garlic cloves that have been sliced and dried. The commercial process in Shandong, the heart of Chinese garlic production, runs through five stages:

  1. Raw material selection. Factories draw on Normal White and Pure White fresh garlic from local storage. Raw garlic quality, firmness, pungency, freedom from disease, sets the ceiling on finished flake quality. Proximity matters: in Shandong, raw material travels from cold store to factory within hours, not days.
  2. Cleaning and peeling. Bulbs are broken into cloves, and the skins are removed mechanically and with controlled water processes. Foreign material and damaged cloves are sorted out.
  3. Slicing. Clean cloves are cut into uniform flakes, typically 1.2-1.8mm thick for standard grade. Slice thickness controls both drying uniformity and the appearance of the finished product.
  4. Hot-air drying. Sliced garlic passes through hot-air (convective) drying tunnels that draw moisture down to around 6%, preserving color, pungency, and flavor compounds while making the product shelf-stable.
  5. Screening and grading. Dried flakes are screened to remove fines and powder, color-sorted to remove dark or blemished pieces, and graded for homogeneity before packing.

The result is a pale golden to light yellow flake with the characteristic sharp garlic aroma. Rehydrated in water for 10-20 minutes, it returns to a soft garlic slice; ground dry, it becomes garlic powder or granules. For a broader look at end uses, see our Dehydrated Garlic Applications article.

Quality Specifications Buyers Should Write Into Contracts

Flake quality is objective and testable. A professional purchase contract specifies at minimum:

  • Moisture: maximum 7%, target 6%. This is the single most important spec. Moisture above 7% invites mold and clumping across a months-long ocean transit and warehouse life. Verification takes minutes with a moisture meter on a drawn sample, or via lab test during third-party inspection.
  • Appearance. Uniform light yellow to golden color, consistent flake size, minimal broken pieces and fines, no dark or burnt flakes, no visible foreign matter.
  • Defect limits. No mold, no insect damage, no rot; limits on blemished or discolored flakes expressed as a maximum percentage.
  • Microbiological indicators. Total plate count, yeast and mold, coliforms, and pathogen limits (such as Salmonella absence) appropriate to your market's food regulations. Buyers supplying the EU, US, or Gulf markets should align specs with destination requirements.
  • Optional analytics. Where flavor consistency is critical, allicin content or pungency indicators can be specified, though most buyers manage this through raw material season and supplier qualification instead.

On inspection: SGS, BV, and similar agencies operate throughout Shandong and can check moisture, grade, and micro indicators before loading, at a cost that is trivial against the value of a rejected container. Making inspection rights contractual is standard practice, and established suppliers expect it. For general supplier vetting, read How to Choose a Garlic Supplier in China.

Packaging and Container Loading

The standard export pack for garlic flakes is 20kg or 25kg per carton with a food-grade inner PE liner bag, heat-sealed to lock out humidity. The liner is not optional detail: flakes at 6% moisture are hygroscopic, and a broken seal in humid transit conditions can lift moisture and cause caking or mold in the affected cartons. Reinforced cartons, palletized and shrink-wrapped, are the norm for container loading.

Custom packaging, smaller packs, private label, or retail-ready formats, can typically be arranged with advance notice. Because garlic flakes are shelf-stable, they generally ship in dry containers rather than reefers, which simplifies logistics compared with fresh garlic (fresh garlic requires a 40HQ reefer at -2 to -3 degrees Celsius; see our container loading guide for that trade). Even so, avoid leaving containers in extreme heat for extended periods; packaging integrity and moisture barriers have limits.

How Garlic Flake Pricing Works

Flake prices are fundamentally a conversion of fresh garlic prices. The processing ratio is roughly 5-6 kg of fresh garlic per 1 kg of finished flakes, depending on raw material moisture and grade. That means:

  • Raw material is the dominant cost driver, typically the majority of the ex-factory price. When fresh garlic moves, flakes move with it, amplified roughly five-fold.
  • The seasonal cycle transfers. Fresh garlic is cheapest in July-August after the June harvest and most expensive in March-May at the end of the storage season. Flake producers who buy raw material cheaply at harvest can hold advantage for months; producers buying raw material in spring pass that cost on. For buyers, the same seasonal logic applies: contract production runs placed in the second half of the year generally price better than spring purchases. See China Garlic Price Trends for the fresh market cycle.
  • Processing costs add a stable layer: energy for hot-air drying, labor, packaging, and factory overhead. These move slowly compared to raw material, so they rarely change the trend, only the level.
  • Grade and spec matter. Tighter color sorting, lower fines, and stricter micro limits all add cost. Paying for exactly the spec your application needs, rather than over-specifying, keeps you competitive.

HS Code, Duties, and Import Paperwork

Dehydrated garlic flakes classify under HS Code 0712.90, dried vegetables, garlic, which is distinct from the fresh garlic heading (0703.20). The distinction matters: duty rates, import licensing, and phytosanitary requirements for dried vegetables differ from fresh garlic in many destinations, often favorably. Confirm the applied tariff line and any certificate requirements (phytosanitary certificate, certificate of origin, health certificate) with your customs broker in your destination market, and see our garlic import regulations by country guide for market-specific details.

Standard export documents from China include the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, and, where required, phytosanitary and health certificates. Suppliers experienced in flake exports generate these routinely; slow or error-prone paperwork is a legitimate reason to move on from a candidate supplier.

Where Garlic Flakes Fit in the Food Industry

Understanding the demand side helps you specify the right product. Flake buyers fall into three broad groups, each with slightly different spec priorities:

  • Seasoning and ingredient processors use flakes as a raw material for spice blends, marinade mixes, soup and sauce bases, and instant meal kits. They buy on consistency: uniform flake size and color so their finished blends look identical batch after batch, plus tight micro limits because their products often face end-market food safety audits.
  • Food manufacturers in meat processing, snack foods, noodles, and ready meals incorporate rehydrated flakes directly into products. Slice integrity after rehydration matters to them, which makes flake thickness and the ratio of whole slices to broken pieces the specs worth negotiating.
  • Food service and distribution chains, catering suppliers, wholesalers, and repackers, buy flakes for long shelf life and kitchen convenience: a 20kg carton stored dry replaces a stream of fresh garlic purchases with no spoilage and no peeling labor. Their priority is packaging durability and moisture reliability over cosmetic grading.

Compared with fresh garlic, flakes also remove cold chain requirements entirely, no reefer, no -2 to -3 degrees Celsius vigilance, no sprouting clock, which is why flake programs are often easier to run in markets with weak refrigerated infrastructure. The trade-off is a longer, more spec-driven buying process on the front end, which is exactly what this guide is designed to shorten.

How to Evaluate Samples Like a Professional

Before any first order, request a sample from current production, 300-500 grams is typical, and run four quick checks that any buyer can perform:

  1. Visual grading on a spread. Pour the sample across a white sheet and estimate the share of broken flakes, fines, and dark pieces against the spec sheet. A five-minute visual beats a container of surprises.
  2. Moisture check. A handheld moisture meter or a lab test confirms the 6% claim. This is the spec most often quietly out of tolerance in cheap quotes.
  3. Rehydration test. Soak a measured portion in warm water for 15-20 minutes. Quality flakes return to soft, pale slices with a strong clean garlic aroma; poor stock rehydrates unevenly or smells flat or off.
  4. Aroma and color consistency versus quote. Match the sample to the grade you were quoted, not the grade you were shown in marketing photos.

Repeat the visual and moisture checks on arrival of every container, and keep retained samples from each lot. If a dispute arises months later, retained samples are your evidence.

A Practical Pre-Purchase Checklist

  1. 1. Fix the spec sheet first. Moisture 7% max (target 6%), flake thickness, color tolerance, fines limit, micro limits matched to your destination market.
  2. 2. Request pre-shipment samples from current production, and test them against the spec, not just look at them.
  3. 3. Confirm packaging: 20kg/25kg cartons, intact PE liners, palletized loading, and the load plan for your container.
  4. 4. Book SGS or BV inspection at loading for moisture and grade, and make the 7% moisture ceiling contractual.
  5. 5. Time the buy: second-half-of-year production runs (post-harvest raw material) historically price better than spring purchases; re-quote before every order.

Bulk flake buying rewards spec discipline more than negotiation skill. The buyers who get consistent containers are the ones who wrote down the numbers before they wired the deposit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between garlic flakes, granules, and powder?
They are the same dried product at different particle sizes. Flakes are the sliced, dried form; granules and powder are produced by milling flakes to controlled mesh ranges. Many buyers import flakes and mill locally for flexibility.
What is the HS code for dehydrated garlic flakes?
0712.90, dried vegetables. This is separate from fresh garlic (0703.20), and duty and documentation requirements often differ between the two categories, so confirm the applied rules with your customs broker before ordering.
How long do dehydrated garlic flakes keep?
At 6% moisture, sealed PE liner, cool and dry storage, flakes hold quality for 12-24 months. Once a carton is opened, use it promptly or reseal it, since flakes absorb humidity quickly and can cake or mold when exposed.
Do garlic flakes need reefer shipping?
No. Properly dried and sealed flakes are shelf-stable and ship in standard dry containers. Just avoid prolonged exposure to extreme heat and humidity, and verify carton and liner integrity at receiving.
How much fresh garlic goes into 1 kg of flakes?
Approximately 5-6 kg of fresh garlic yields 1 kg of dehydrated flakes, depending on raw material moisture and grade. This ratio is why flake pricing tracks the fresh garlic market so closely.
