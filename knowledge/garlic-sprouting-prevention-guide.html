Garlic Sprouting Prevention: A Complete Guide for Importers and Distributors

Why garlic sprouts, what a broken cold chain costs you, and the exact temperatures, checks, and routines that keep bulbs dormant from Shandong cold store to your customer's shelf.

Published: October 11, 2026  |  Reading time: 12 minutes  |  By ExCrop (Heneng International Trade Group, Shandong)

Quick Answer: Garlic sprouts when its 60-90 day natural dormancy ends and temperatures rise above refrigeration levels. You cannot reverse sprouting, only prevent it. Prevention means one thing above all: never let the bulb warm up. Keep garlic at -2 to -3 degrees Celsius from cold store through reefer voyage, move it into 0-4 degrees Celsius storage immediately on arrival, and verify sprout status (germ check) before you pay for the container.

  • Natural dormancy after harvest: 60-90 days at ambient temperature
  • Cold chain set point: -2 to -3 degrees Celsius, unchanged from storage to container
  • Post-arrival storage: 0-4 degrees Celsius, humidity 60-70%
  • Contract sprouting tolerance: typically 1-2% maximum visible sprouts

A single green shoot emerging from a garlic bulb can undo months of careful sourcing. Sprouted garlic loses weight and firmness, turns bitter around the green germ, gets downgraded by wholesalers, and gets rejected by retailers. For importers, sprouting is the most common quality claim on garlic containers, and in most cases it is preventable. This guide covers the biology, the logistics, the inspection standards, and a practical checklist you can apply to your very next shipment.

Why Garlic Sprouts in the First Place

Garlic is a storage organ. The bulb exists to fuel the next generation of the plant, and inside every clove sits a dormant embryo (the germ or sprout) programmed to grow when conditions say it is time. After harvest, the bulb enters a true dormancy phase lasting roughly 60-90 days, during which the embryo stays asleep even at room temperature. That is why freshly harvested Chinese garlic shipped in July and August arrives looking perfect regardless of handling.

The problems start when dormancy ends. From roughly October onward, the same crop becomes temperature-sensitive: sustained warmth above about 20-25 degrees Celsius triggers rapid sprout development, while cool-but-not-cold conditions (5-15 degrees Celsius) ironically provide the classic "winter has passed" signal that many bulbs interpret as spring. This is why garlic stored in a cool basement can sprout faster than expected, and why the commercial solution is not just "cool" but genuinely cold: -2 to -3 degrees Celsius, which keeps the germ dormant and preserves dry matter for 8-10 months.

Timing also matters. Late-season stock (March-May) has already spent most of its dormancy budget, so it sprouts far more eagerly when warmed than early-season stock does. A container that arrives sprout-free in November can tolerate a few days of handling; the same handling in April can be fatal.

What Sprouting Actually Costs You

Sprouting is not cosmetic. Once the germ elongates, it consumes the reserves stored in the clove:

  • Dry matter loss. The sprout grows at the expense of the clove's stored carbohydrates. The bulb visibly loses firmness and weight.
  • Flavor degradation. Green germ tissue is bitter; consumers and food processors must cut it out, wasting product and labor.
  • Commercial downgrading. Most export contracts cap visible sprouting at 1-2% of bulbs. Beyond that, the lot is rejected or re-priced, and claims between buyer and seller consume weeks.
  • Accelerated spoilage. Sprouted bulbs respire more, generate moisture and heat, and pull surrounding bulbs into a deterioration cascade inside closed packaging.

In short: prevention is cheap, remediation is impossible, and claims are slow. Everything that follows is prevention.

Cold Chain Control: Where Sprouting Is Won or Lost

Between the Shandong cold store and your warehouse, garlic passes through several temperature environments, and each transition is a sprouting risk. The export-standard chain looks like this:

  • Cold store: -2 to -3 degrees Celsius, 65-70% humidity. Garlic harvested in June is stabilized here from July onward. A well-run store is the foundation of everything downstream. Ask suppliers for their temperature records, not verbal assurances.
  • Tempering and loading. Bulbs are tempered 24-72 hours before loading to avoid condensation inside packaging (see our Cold Stored Garlic Guide for details), then loaded into the reefer in the coolest hours of the day.
  • Ocean transit: reefer set to -2 to -3 degrees Celsius. A 25-40 day voyage at the same temperature as the cold store means the garlic never experiences a warming event. This is the single most important specification in your booking.
  • Discharge and inland transport. The riskiest hours of the whole journey. A container that sits on the quay for 3 days in 35 degrees Celsius heat, or is trucked without power for two days, accumulates irreversible sprout and mold pressure. Plan discharge, pre-clear documents, and keep inland legs short and cold.

A broken chain cannot be repaired. Once bulbs warm past dormancy and sprouting initiates, returning them to cold slows further growth but does not restore lost dry matter. Importers who understand this treat cold chain continuity as a contractual requirement, not a courtesy. Storage After Arrival: Protecting the Bulbs in Your Custody

When the container arrives, your own storage decisions take over. The right regime depends on how fast you turn the stock:

  • 0-4 degrees Celsius (refrigerated warehouse): the standard for imported stock that will sell over the following weeks to months. At this range sprouting stays suppressed and quality holds. Keep humidity around 60-70%, dry enough to prevent mold, humid enough to avoid excessive weight loss.
  • -2 to -3 degrees Celsius (deep cold): the choice if you are holding stock for several months, replicating the export cold store. Note that taking garlic back down to this range means managing condensation again whenever you draw stock out for sale.
  • Ambient storage: only acceptable for quick-turnover stock in cool seasons, and only within the 60-90 day dormancy window of a new crop. Never store post-dormancy cold stored garlic at ambient temperature; expect green shoots within days to weeks at 20-25 degrees Celsius.

Two more receiving rules: first, go into cold storage immediately on arrival, the dock-to-chiller gap should be hours, not days; second, ventilate. Stacked garlic respires; stagnant humid air in the middle of a pallet stack breeds mold even at correct temperature. Checking Sprout Status Before You Pay

Prevention starts at purchase. Sprouting that has already begun inside the clove is invisible from outside the bulb, so serious buyers check the germ directly:

  1. Sample randomly. Pull 50-100 bulbs from different bags and positions, not the showcase top layer.
  2. Cut crosswise. Slice bulbs horizontally through the middle of the cloves to expose the germ cavity.
  3. Read the germ. A dormant germ is small, pale and flat against the clove. An activated germ is elongated, thicker, sometimes green-tinged, and pushing toward the clove tip. The proportion of activated germs in the sample predicts how the lot will behave when warmed.
  4. Write it into the contract. Specify maximum visible sprouting (1-2% is the export norm) and the right to third-party inspection (SGS, BV) before loading. Suppliers who store properly are happy to prove it; hesitation tells you something.

For an overview of other pre-shipment checks (moisture, mold, calibre), see our cold stored garlic inspection section. For supplier evaluation generally, read How to Choose a Garlic Supplier in China.

The 5-Point Sprout Prevention Checklist

  1. 1. Ship the full journey at -2 to -3 degrees Celsius. Cold store to reefer, same set point, no exceptions. Confirm the reefer set point in writing before loading, and verify the container's temperature log on arrival.
  2. 2. Avoid long summer routes on late-season stock. A March-May shipment through 40 degrees Celsius heat at transshipment ports multiplies risk on garlic whose dormancy is nearly spent. Prefer direct services, and consider moving big volumes into Q4-Q1 bookings instead.
  3. 3. Receive into cold storage the same day. Pre-clear customs documents before vessel arrival, book your chiller space in advance, and minimize the container's time unpowered at the port. Every warm hour is irreversible.
  4. 4. Hold stock at 0-4 degrees Celsius, 60-70% humidity, with airflow. Rotate first-in-first-out, avoid stacking against walls, and monitor for moisture pockets that precede mold.
  5. 5. Verify sprout status before paying. Germ-cut a 50-100 bulb random sample, or book SGS/BV inspection at loading. Make the 1-2% sprouting tolerance contractual, especially for March-May shipments.

None of these five points is expensive. All of them are habit. The importers who rarely file garlic quality claims are the ones who run this checklist on every single shipment, from every supplier, every season.

Three Real-World Sprouting Failures and What Caused Them

Most sprouting claims trace back to a small number of repeatable mistakes. Learning to recognize them is faster than learning them firsthand:

  • The port delay. A 40HQ reefer discharges at a congested destination port and sits unpowered on the quay for 4-5 days in 35 degrees Celsius heat. The pulp temperature climbs 15-20 degrees, and by the time the container is delivered, germ elongation has begun in a meaningful share of bulbs. The reefer log shows the voyage itself was flawless, which is why claims become disputes. Prevention: pre-clear documents, and know your port's dwell statistics before booking.
  • The "warm set point" decision. A forwarder, trying to avoid condensation complaints, sets the reefer to +2 degrees Celsius instead of -2 to -3 degrees Celsius. Over a 30-day voyage, that difference is enough to wake a significant share of germs in post-dormancy stock. Prevention: specify the set point in the booking contract and verify the container's download log at delivery.
  • The delayed warehouse intake. The container arrives on Thursday, but the buyer's cold room is full until Monday. Four days on an ambient dock in a warm market, and the top layers of each pallet begin sprouting and sweating within a week. Prevention: schedule intake capacity with the same discipline as the vessel arrival.

Notice the pattern: in all three cases the garlic itself was fine when loaded. Sprouting risk is mostly a logistics discipline problem, which is good news, because discipline is something you can specify, contract, and verify.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I eat sprouted garlic?
Sprouted garlic is safe to eat once the bitter green germ is removed, but it is inferior product: softer, lighter, and less flavorful. For trade purposes, visible sprouting above 1-2% of bulbs breaches typical export tolerances and triggers downgrades or claims.
Why did my garlic sprout in the fridge?
Household fridges run 4-8 degrees Celsius, which slows sprouting but does not stop it, and the humid environment can encourage root and mold growth. Commercial suppression requires -2 to -3 degrees Celsius. At typical fridge temperatures expect slower sprouting, not zero sprouting.
Does cold storage harm the garlic?
No, when done correctly. At -2 to -3 degrees Celsius garlic holds firmness and quality for 8-10 months. Damage occurs only if the store runs below about -4 degrees Celsius (freeze damage: water-soaked, translucent cloves) or if humidity and ventilation are mismanaged.
How do I know if garlic is already sprouting inside?
Cut sample bulbs crosswise and inspect the germ: dormant germs are small, pale and flat; activated germs are elongated, thicker, possibly green-tinged and pushing toward the clove tip. The share of activated germs across a 50-100 bulb sample tells you how the lot will behave when warmed.
Which months carry the highest sprouting risk?
March through May, when storage stock has spent most of its dormancy, plus any shipment routed through high summer heat. October-December shipments on early storage-season stock carry the least risk.
